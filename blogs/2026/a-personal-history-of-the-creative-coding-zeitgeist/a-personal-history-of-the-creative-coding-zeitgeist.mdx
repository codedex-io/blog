## The Spark

I will be honest: I have been feeling a sense of malaise about the state of [Creative Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creative_technology). 

Yikes. It feels strange – almost forbidden – to say that out loud. I'm a tech educator, after all.

_Where is the spark I once felt about the space? Is this just a classic kind of nostalgia that hits you deeper in your work, when you're several years removed from that early-career idealism?_

Lately, I've wanted to excavate the spark that got me into Creative Tech in the first place.

To do that, I'm going to dive into a history. I'm calling this **A Personal History** rather than _The_ History, since I'm just one Julien. :~)

## The Book of Ideas

To set the stage, I'm going to ask you a few questions:

- _Is interactivity magic?_ <sup>1</sup>
- _If the internet just stopped working one day, what's the first thing we would miss?_ <sup>2</sup>
- _How can we build systems that embody more than just human agendas?_ <sup>3</sup>

These are all quotes from artists who contributed to the _[Eyebeam Book of Ideas (2016)](https://www.eyebeam.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Eyebeam-Book-of-Ideas-1.pdf)_. 

Specifically, these are from <sup>1</sup> [CHiKa](https://imagima.com), <sup>2</sup> [Joanne McNeil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joanne_McNeil), and <sup>3</sup> [Tega Brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tega_Brain).

Before I moved to New York, I visited the city a handful of times. In 2017, the summer before my senior year of college, I came to visit a dear friend. [Eyebeam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyebeam_(organization)), an NYC nonprofit arts organization, happened to be moving to a new location, and they were holding a studio yard sale. I dug through bins and bins of nuts, bolts, and microcontrollers, with an actual shovel.

<Caption text="Ancient artifacts at the yard sale in Industry City, Brooklyn; CD box."/>

In these piles of detritus, I found old CD-ROMs and even some floppy disks. And, most importantly, I found the _[Eyebeam Book of Ideas (2016)](https://www.eyebeam.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Eyebeam-Book-of-Ideas-1.pdf)_, a book of ideas, queries, and meditations by artists who had completed Eyebeam's residency program.

I opened it, and was struck by a familiar brain-buzzing feeling. I felt such a sense of possibility, looking at the recent history of Making, thinking through my own potential future of Making.

I was panicking about what life would look like after I graduated college in 2018, oscillating between trying to commercialize my design practice to land a "real" gig, and leaning into the weirdo creative tech stuff I held so dearly. I figured it out about as much as I could without smoothing out my edges too much. At least not forever. More on that later.

So, what has shifted between then and now? What came before?

Time to investigate. 🕵️‍♂️

## History Time

The Creative Technology industry, like any other industry, goes through trend cycles. Creative Tech is unique in its position between \~Art & Technology\~. It encompasses art, design, tech, computation, academia, software engineering, commercial production, and ✨more✨.

You can check out a more complete [Creative Tech Timeline](https://github.com/laserpilot/creative-tech-timeline) by Blair Neal, but for the sake of this blog, I'm going to talk about what has most intimately and immediately formed my world.

So where did it all begin?

### MIT Media Lab

The **MIT Media Lab**, founded in 1985, has taken on a nearly mythological quality for engineers and designers alike. It's made up of specialized research groups that graduate students can apply to for hyper-focused studies. They all have super interesting names like Mediated Matter, Opera of the Future, Lifelong Kindergarten, Sculpting Evolution, and so many more.

[Muriel Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muriel_Cooper) was a pioneering designer who was MIT Media Lab's co-founder, as well as the Design Director for the MIT Press. At the time of her death in 1994, she was the first female tenured professor at the lab.

![Bauhaus poster](https://github.com/codedex-io/blog/blob/main/blogs/2026/a-personal-history-of-the-creative-coding-zeitgeist/assets/poster.webp?raw=true)
<Caption text="Muriel Cooper, Poster to promote The Bauhaus. (1969)"/>

The Lab has evolved to more explicitly support creative coding with the founding of the [Future Sketches](https://www.media.mit.edu/groups/future-sketches/overview/) research group, dedicated to "Exploring the essence of code as a creative medium."

Among the many experiments to come out of the Media Lab, one would become particularly important to creative coding: Processing.

### Processing & p5.js

My first exposure to creative coding was [Processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processing), the first widely used programming language for artists, invented by Casey Reas and Ben Fry in 2001 while they were graduate students at the Aesthetics and Computation Group at the Lab. Processing was inspired by John Maeda's 1999 project, [Design By Numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_By_Numbers), developed in the same research group. 

<img src="https://github.com/codedex-io/blog/blob/main/blogs/2026/a-personal-history-of-the-creative-coding-zeitgeist/assets/processing.webp?raw=true" alt="21 Processing examples" width="75%"></img>
<Caption text="Some early Processing examples from 2003."/>

I stumbled across Processing in 2014 after a lifetime of avoiding coding (I'm an artist! I don't code…), and quickly found myself making my [very first game](https://www.codedex.io/projects/build-a-conversational-game-in-p5js) with the language.

Processing led to the development of [p5.js](https://p5js.org/people) in 2013, a JavaScript framework created by Lauren Lee McCarthy that brought the functionality of Processing to the web. This took Processing from something that could only exist locally (with occasional ports to the web) to a fully fledged JavaScript library. It could now be seamlessly integrated into websites of all kinds.

Much like the smartphone democratized photography, Processing and p5.js democratized programming. Artists now had a carefully crafted framework to express their ideas through code, without needing a CS education.

Processing exists within an ecosystem of creative coding. Here is a diagram from Casey Reas's [2018 blog post](https://medium.com/processing-foundation/a-modern-prometheus-59aed94abe85), connecting Processing to other languages you may have learned in school:

## Academia

I've walked through how creative coding languages are born out of academic incubators, but how can students navigate academia if they're interested in creative coding?

Academic programs (especially undergraduate programs) often respond to what the industry requires, which itself is responding to the broader economy and venture capital funding and social trends aaaaand on and on.

Big loop! [Ouroboros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouroboros) as hell!

<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/71/Serpiente_alquimica.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=imageinfo&utm_content=thumbnail_unscaled" alt="Ouroboros" width="70%"></img>
<Caption text="♾️. (Wikipedia)"/>

We can see this loop playing out at two schools: UCLA Design Media Arts & NYU ITP.

### UCLA Design Media Arts

**[UCLA Design Media Arts](https://www.design.ucla.edu)** (my alma mater) is an experimental bachelor's program that teaches design as a form of expression, a tool for artmaking, rather than as a commercial practice. 

Creative coding is a cornerstone of the major, and Processing and p5.js play a major role in its foundational programming classes. Casey Reas (Processing) joined the faculty in 2003 and Lauren McCarthy (p5.js) joined in 2016; both were major flashpoints for the focus of the program and significant motivators for me to attend.

DMA recently announced that they'll be splitting their degree into [3 separate bachelor's degree programs](https://www.design.ucla.edu/events/announcements/dma-launches-three-new-majors). When I attended, it was one huge interdisciplinary program with 3 concentrations:
- Visual Communication & Image
- Video & Animation
- Interactivity & Games

These concentrations were all under one bachelor's degree program. The concentrations only applied to students' senior thesis classes but were not formally reflected on their degrees.

The new structure will be 3 distinct BA degrees:
- [Design](https://www.design.ucla.edu/programs/design) (encompassing visual design and motion)
- [Games](https://www.design.ucla.edu/programs/games) (its own field separate from Interactivity)
- [Computational Art](https://www.design.ucla.edu/programs/computational-art) (ding ding ding! Our creative coding degree).

DMA also has an MFA program, but it is more fine arts/gallery focused, though students use games and creative technology as a framework for their practice.

### NYU ITP

**[NYU ITP](https://tisch.nyu.edu/itp)** (Interactive Telecommunications Program) was a graduate program founded in 1979 by [Red Burns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_Burns), known as the "Godmother of Silicon Alley" (yes, that's *Alley*, not *Valley*) of NYC. It was initially founded with the goal of directly connecting citizens to the technology they were using, inspired by a 1970s demonstration Burns saw of the Sony [Portapak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portapak). 

It was the first grad program to bridge the gap between laypeople, technology, and creative expression. And while the technologies have changed, its emphasis on computation as a medium for creative and artistic expression has remained.

In the process, ITP was a major force in establishing the Creative Technologist as a discipline – it is often described as "an art school for engineers, and an engineering school for artists".

ITP has an undergraduate counterpart called **IMA** (Interactive Media Arts), a BFA launched at NYU's New York campus in 2018 in response to an increasing demand for undergraduate creative technology education.

I think of NYU ITP as the more explicitly applied creative technology program, and UCLA DMA as a more expansive experimental/research-based program. As a private school, NYU markets itself as more heavily tied to industry.

P.S.! Obviously there are plenty of other programs, but I am focusing on the most influential players in my personal history.

## Can You Vibe Code Learning?

Now that we've discussed the history of creative coding, I need to complain for a second.

I've been circling the drain on three major areas: vibe coding vs. actual learning, the gap between academia → industry, and The Industry™️ itself.

So, can you vibe code _learning_?

Nah, but [teaching is weird right now](https://garden3d.substack.com/p/can-you-vibe-code-learning).

A lot of the time, when I tell people I teach coding, they immediately ask if/how the tech industry is changing because of AI. **No shade, but it feels like a kneejerk question, almost obligatory, the way your aunt might ask if you're still single during the holidays.** Ok, maybe not that bad.

<img src="https://github.com/codedex-io/blog/blob/main/blogs/2026/a-personal-history-of-the-creative-coding-zeitgeist/assets/clippy.jpeg?raw=true" alt="biblically accurate clippy tweet" width="70%"></img>
<Caption text="Clippy pls save us 🪽"/>


But it really is weird. Every Codédex exercise I write, I'm accounting for the fact that people could easily use AI as a shortcut and never actually absorb anything. I try to justify my own work by reminding myself that there is still value in [Coding The Web From Scratch](https://www.codedex.io/blog/coding-the-web-from-scratch).

People who want to understand the code they're writing will _do the work_ to actually learn. And they continue to! Despite the increasing temptation to outsource technical thinking to a robot.

## Academia → Industry

I have felt conflicted about academic programs that market themselves as launchpads for a Creative Technologist career. 

The kinds of projects people make in school are expansive, unusual, and unconventional.

However, with mounting economic pressure and a rapidly changing industry, I worry that the marketing of these programs does not accurately reflect the reality of working in the industry itself. 

NYU ITP's motto, listed on its admissions page, states "ITP does not train people for their first jobs after graduation. We train people for their last jobs." I wonder if this puts new graduates in a difficult position.

UCLA DMA taught me how to _think_ – people often say the program prepares us to be conceptual art directors, but not designers in the conventional sense. I had to work tirelessly to position myself to land full-time jobs. And I wouldn't trade it for anything.

But! Inside me, there are two wolves:

- One says that school should be a place to dream wildly, to make projects that can live only within the container of an experimental program. I feel incredibly grateful to have been able to experiment in this way myself.
- The other part of me worried that students are taking on massive amounts of student loan debt to enter an industry which is no longer the thing that was promised to them.

It feels taboo, even gauche, to discuss your economic reality when you are in art school, but it is necessary. It does students a disservice to ignore it, and this impact is only magnified if they're from a marginalized background.

Don't get me started on what happens when new grads enter the industry. Or, actually, do.

## \~The Industry\~

Oh, the industry. As a bright-eyed and busy-tailed undergraduate, I wanted nothing more than to become a Creative Technologist. In my mind, this would entail making a living from creative directing interactive art for clients who wish to be delighted. 

The industry itself turned out to be pretty different.

I remember I had a UX interview for one of the FAANGs 🧛 my senior year, and I confidently explained that the games in my portfolio were designed to be mysterious and not entirely knowable. The engineer on the other line was silent. You can see why I didn't move forward. 

The industry can be extractive toward new grads who want a creative career. It often eats itself. This has contributed wildly to my malaise.

In my time working, I've observed the following progression:

[Microsites](https://amessagefrom.earth/) → [Interactive Installations](https://www.bluecadet.com/work/the-met-81st-st-studio) → Augmented Reality (AR) → Mixed Reality (MR) → Trend cycle falls off → AI Boom → ?????? 

We'll see what's next!

Hiring cycles have followed the ups and downs of the industry. Creative Tech teams at agencies sit on the bench and then get laid off and get rehired, first as freelancers and then as full-timers depending on where the industry is in the trend cycle.

If you look at the moodboards for these agencies, they're often digging through Pinterest for decontextualized/decapitated inspo. 

Blair Neal's [Advice for Creative Technologists](https://ablairneal.com/advice-for-creative-technologists) has an excellent bit about how CTs can ethically work with Creative Technology when they are inspired by artists without ripping them off.

> Respect the arts and artists. There are a lot of parallels with projects you’ll work on and things going on in the art world. Make sure you’re taking time to gather inspiration from those works and uses of technology. However, there is a fine line between feeling inspired by something and ripping it off – make sure you’re following good ethics. If you find yourself getting too close to an existing work, work with your team to change the direction – or reach out to the original artist directly. Make sure you give back to the arts community as well – donate money or time, host meet ups, do some writing, contribute to open source initiatives, etc.

### Influencer Culture

I've been seeing a lot of Creative Tech influencers lately! On one hand I am excited that people are making new things and sharing them. On the other hand, it makes me nervous to see these things in the contextlessness of The Algorithm.

For example, creators like [[cc]](https://bimbotech.co/cyberdeck/) and [Annike Tan](https://www.tiktok.com/@ubeboobey) are making [cyberdecks](https://www.teenvogue.com/story/diy-cyberdecks-newest-analog-trend-taking-social-media) again, which I love so much! ## Hopeful Conclusion

### Brain Buzz 2.0

So... I've been searching for the spark again.

A friend reminded me of [CLOUDS](https://vimeo.com/917357948), an interactive documentary made in 2014, capturing the state of Creative Technology at the time. The film interviewed 40 artists and technologists, but rather than just taking video, it captured their interviews immersively using depth cameras and openFrameworks.

The open source code was literally [released on GitHub](https://github.com/CLOUDS-Interactive-Documentary/CLOUDS), which was SO rad. I had seen this years ago and forgot about it. I felt that old brain buzz feeling appear at the back of my neck. I'm heartened to know it's still there.
