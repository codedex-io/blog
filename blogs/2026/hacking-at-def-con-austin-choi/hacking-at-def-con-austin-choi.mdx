## Hacking.

A word that people fear, or at least keep their distance from. But what does it really mean?

At its core, hacking is simply about being curious. It's about looking at something, figuring out how it works, what makes it tick, and discovering what you can do with it.

That was me this last summer, surrounded by 30,000+ other people doing the exact same thing, at [DEF CON](https://defcon.org), the world's largest and most iconic hacking conference, held annually in Las Vegas.

### Origin Story

So for those of you who don't know, DEF CON was started by [Jeff "The Dark Tangent" Moss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeff_Moss_(hacker)) back in 1993 as a way to say farewell to a hacker friend of his. He brought 100 hackers with him to Las Vegas so they could party together.

Somehow, this ended up becoming a tradition that eventually grew into what it is today: a conference that boasts tens of thousands of attendees from all over the world, representing companies like Google and Microsoft, government agencies like the FBI, NSA, DOE, and CISA, and even hacker groups that will go unnamed.

### The Name

Where did the name DEF CON even come from, some of you may wonder. 

Some of you in the US may know about [DEFCON](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DEFCON) in the military, which stands for the Defense Readiness Condition, a scale to indicate the level of military threat against the US. Jeff Moss took this after it got popularized in the 1983 movie, _WarGames_. 

He also cites that he used the old phone keypad where the number 3 would represent the letters "DEF" as an homage to old school [phone phreakers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_box), people who hacked phone lines. "Con" is just short for conference, nothing really deep here lol.

<Quote text="Hacking is simply about being curious. It's about looking at something, figuring out how it works, what makes it tick, and discovering what you can do with it." />

DEF CON is usually held in the first week or second week of August, which is also known as the Hacker Summer Camp, a week with 3 of the world's biggest cybersecurity conferences all in Las Vegas: BSidesLV, Black Hat, and DEF CON. 

Nowadays, DEF CON calls the [Las Vegas Convention Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Vegas_Convention_Center) home. But years before, it would be held across multiple casinos like the Caesars Forum, the LINQ, Harrah's, etc.

### The Villages

DEF CON is split into what are called Villages. 

**Villages** are groups of people that share interests in subcategories of cybersecurity. 

For example:

- **Red Team Village:** A village all about offensive security and hacking.
- **Crypto and Privacy Village:** A group of math obsessed people trying to obfuscate whatever they can.
- **Aerospace Village:** A space full of planes and rockets that you can actually hack.
- **Car Hacking Village:** The same as Aerospace but with cars lol.
- **Lockpicking Village:** Where you can learn how to be like the Lock Picking Lawyer (just maybe not as good).
- **Badge Life Village:** Where people share the badges they made and collected over the years (more on badges later).

### How did I get here?

Thanks to my involvement with the UNLV cybersecurity orgs, I managed to go to DEF CON for the first time in 2023 where I got my first taste of real hacker culture. I got to experience CTFs (Capture the Flag competitions, more on those later), Workshops, the Wall of Sheep which shamed people who logged into things over the conference wi-fi without encryption by putting their usernames and blurred passwords on a giant screen, and so much more. 

It was an absolute blast and I couldn't get enough. So, ever since then, I ended up going several more years.

### Badges

So you might be wondering, what's so important about badges that warrants an entire village?

Well badges aren't simply a way to get into the conference, they're a way of life. Back in DEF CON 14 (2006), Jeff Moss decided to spice things up a bit and introduced the first DEF CON electronic badge.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/codedex-io/blog/refs/heads/main/blogs/2026/hacking-at-def-con-austin-choi/assets/badge.png" alt="Badge" width="100%"></img>

Ever since then, every other year, DEF CON issues out electronic badges that come with some cool features that attendees can hack with, hack into, and just mess around with. I actually have 2 electronic badges myself, and this year's badge is one of them.

Thanks to this, people started creating their own custom electronic badges with cool features. Some of these include badges like a wearable Pip-Boy badge, a laser tag badge, a Flock camera blocking badge, and so much more.

I managed to wrangle myself a decent collection just from this year.

<img src="https://raw.githubusercontent.com/codedex-io/blog/refs/heads/main/blogs/2026/hacking-at-def-con-austin-choi/assets/lanyards.png" alt="" width="100%"></img>

I even got a badge that acts like a D20. Guess I know what I'll be using next D&D night.

My favorite badge from this year though, was that interesting looking badge with a numpad on it. This was the "5N4CK3Y" (SNACKEY) badge that people can only get by completing a CTF that makes you "hack" into a vending machine.

### Capture the Flag!!!

One of the staples in the cybersecurity community is CTFs. 

**Capture the Flag (CTF)** are competitions where people solve cybersecurity challenges and submit flags in order to get points or reach some objective. DEF CON is full of them and they can range anywhere from the most beginner friendly ones to extremely complex ones that would even make Claude Fable and GPT-6 Astra curl up into the fetal position on their therapist's couch.

5N4CK3Y was one that some friends and I did in order to win the 5N4CK3Y badge. It consisted of radio frequency challenges, cryptography, and many more topics.

Another CTF I did, and absolutely loved, was "Gold Bug" by Crypto and Privacy Village, where attendees roleplay as a detective to solve a mystery. These are easily some of the most difficult cryptography challenges I've ever come across, granted that isn't saying much. It consisted of challenges where you have to analyze an irl art gallery the village set up in order to solve puzzles online. I barely made any progress at all so eh, here's to next year.

As much as I wanted to compete in more challenges, I couldn't get to all of the ones I wanted to. But there was one I didn't do that I would argue is one of my favorite CTFs at the entire conference, the "? Cube", created by Las Vegas' very own DEF CON group DC702.

### Nothing like hanging out with friends

Over the last year, I got close with the local Las Vegas DEF CON group, going to their weekly meetings and just having fun. Thanks to this I just got to hang out and have fun after the conference hours ended, just talking, drinking some soju, and just relaxing. One thing many new DEF CON attendees tend to forget is that DEF CON started as a party, and is still at its core a party, just a really big one now.

I'd be hanging out with the DC702 guys, friends from school, and random strangers from all walks of life. We would go to the dance floor and enjoy the music, go to a hotel room to play some pool, and just enjoy ourselves.

### Coding at DEF CON!

So you might be thinking, this is all cool and all, but I'm an engineer, not a hacker, what does any of this have to do with me?

Now here's the thing, hacking IS coding, just pointed in a different direction. Instead of writing code to build something, you're writing code to figure out how something already built works, and then poking at it until it does something it wasn't supposed to do.

Almost every CTF challenge I attempted got me writing a script or two at some point. You get handed a weird file, a server to talk to, or a blob of encrypted data, and you pull out [Python](https://codedex.io/python) to start chipping away at it. `pwntools` for talking to servers, `PyCryptodome` for the crypto stuff, and a lot of `print()` functions, because at 2am in the middle of a CTF, print debugging becomes your best friend.

Badges are the same story. The cool electronic gadgets hanging around your neck are just tiny computers running firmware that somebody wrote, usually in C, MicroPython, etc., and you can go ahead and mess around with them and even flash your own firmware onto them. Half of the Badge Life Village is people showing off code they wrote to make a piece of plastic blink in a way that nobody really asked for.

And then there's my talk, which was essentially me standing in front of a room full of hackers for 30 minutes explaining how software developers should be writing their code, which still feels a little backwards to say out loud.

So if you're on Codédex right now grinding through courses like [The Legend of Python](https://www.codedex.io/python), you're already way closer to this world than you think. The hackers at DEF CON aren't wizards performing magic, they're just people who got curious about what their code could do to someone else's code.

If you ever have the chance to attend DEF CON whether it's from work, volunteering, or presenting, take that chance and GO.
